# Study time: products, communities, and evidence

> Public neighbors of the study-time proposal, from spaced repetition and read-it-later tools to AI tutoring and cognitive-training claims.

Topic: `study-time-landscape`
Glossary: omit

Sources checked on 2026-10-17. Product descriptions below come from their
public documentation, not hands-on comparative testing. This is a targeted
landscape for the [proposal](study-time.md), not an exhaustive market census.

## Current products and open tools

| Project | Publicly described capability | Relationship to study time |
|---|---|---|
| [RemNote Guided Learn](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/15724936-guided-learn-mode) | Turns documents, PDFs, videos, or existing notes into section summaries, flashcards, quizzes, and a guided study plan; includes interactive explanations. | Close overlap after a source is selected. Our proposed entry point infers what is worth studying from work and conversations, then shows a toy demo. |
| [Recall](https://www.recall.it/) | Saves and summarizes media and documents, connects them in a knowledge graph, and provides chat, quizzes, and spaced repetition. | Close capture-to-learning neighbor. Contextual selection should earn its value against this, rather than claiming the whole loop is new. |
| [Readwise Reader](https://readwise.io/read) | Read-it-later environment with annotation and Ghostreader assistance for questions, definitions, and simplification. | A strong existing home for saved reading; a study compiler could point into it instead of rebuilding a reader. |
| [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/) | Community-developed, open-source desktop flashcards, shared decks, add-ons, and scheduled review. | The established retention ecosystem to acknowledge and interoperate with selectively. It need not become the entire learning experience. |
| [FSRS in Anki](https://docs.ankiweb.net/manual/deck-options) | A review scheduler with desired-retention controls and parameters optimized from review history. | Retention scheduling after learning; interest-based “Later” is a different signal. |
| [Orbit](https://github.com/andymatuschak/orbit) | Experimental platform for repeated tasks embedded in texts, developed around the mnemonic medium; public source, Apache-licensed libraries and AGPL/Business Source alternatives for the application and service. | Particularly relevant to an explanation followed by meaningful interaction. Its README explicitly emphasizes research over a polished general-purpose product. |
| [Mnemosyne](https://mnemosyne-proj.org/) | Free flashcard tool with adaptive review and an opt-in long-term-memory research component. | Another community-oriented retention predecessor; its homepage is a functional description, not evidence for broad cognitive gains. |

The working differentiation is activity-derived invitations, enough context
to explain why a topic matters, and a concrete demonstration before asking
for further commitment. It remains a design hypothesis: these sources do not
establish that no existing product offers that combination.

## A longer tradition of using tools to learn

SuperMemo's [incremental reading](https://super-memory.com/help/read.htm)
already combines imported material, selective reading, extracts, and later
review. This is a direct ancestor for returning to worthwhile material over
time, rather than treating a reading backlog as a collection to finish.
The developer's broader effectiveness claims are not adopted here.

Michael Nielsen's [“Augmenting Long-term Memory”](https://augmentingcognition.com/ltm.html)
describes using Anki while learning technical material and making connections.
Anki's forums, shared decks, translations, and add-on contributors matter:
the practice is a community of learners adapting tools, not just an algorithm.
Andy Matuschak's Orbit and its work with Nielsen on the mnemonic medium
extend the question to explanations designed around later recall.

[Quantified Self](https://quantifiedself.com/about/) supplies another nearby
tradition: learning from one's own observations and data. For study time,
that suggests trying the practice and noticing what it changes, while
avoiding a productivity dashboard whose numbers become the objective.

## The brain-training caution

Dual n-back enthusiasm did have scientific antecedents; it was not invented
from nothing. The literature includes positive claims and disputed transfer.
The relevant distinction is between improving a practiced task, improving
related tasks, and improving substantially different abilities.

In an experimental test, Thompson et al. reported improvement on trained
tasks without the hoped-for general cognitive gains
([2013, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0063614)).
Melby-Lervåg, Redick, and Hulme's meta-analysis of 87 publications and 145
comparisons found no convincing reliable far-transfer gains against treated
controls, despite improvements in working-memory measures
([2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033/)). Those findings
do not establish that every cognitive exercise is useless or settle every
population and intervention.

Study time should promise learning the chosen material and making its uses
recognizable. A successful toy exercise does not establish higher general
intelligence, and familiarity with an explanation does not establish transfer.
For a broader benefit, check an independently chosen new problem, delayed
understanding, or a concrete supervisory decision. Enjoying an exercise is
also a sufficient personal reason to do it; it needs no cognitive-upgrade claim.
