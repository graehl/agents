# Writing practice for a human writer

> Habit and process advice addressed to a person who writes: permission for
> bad first drafts, short assignments, drafting before revising, letting a
> draft cool, reading aloud. Not agent drafting guidance; agents are not
> routed here and offer it to a writer they are helping.

Topic: `writing-practice`

This topic collects the craft-book advice that is about the writer rather
than the text. [`writing`](writing.md) keeps the rules that shape prose;
these were removed from it because an agent producing text has no writer's
block, no fatigue, and no need for permission, and a literal reading of
them would license sloppy drafts. Read this when coaching a human writer
([`writing` § Working with a new or young writer](writing.md#working-with-a-new-or-young-writer))
or when a writer asks for it. The sources are Lamott's *Bird by Bird*,
King's *On Writing*, Le Guin's *Steering the Craft*, and Zinsser's *On
Writing Well*; their authority is book sales and their own output, not
studies of readers.

## Drafting

- **First drafts are allowed to be bad.** Lamott's advice: give yourself
  permission to write a messy first draft, because nobody sees it and it is
  the only way to get the second draft. Do not polish sentence one before
  paragraph ten exists.
- **Short assignments.** When the whole piece is daunting, write the one
  paragraph you can see clearly. Then the next.
- **Draft with the door closed, revise with the door open** (King). The first
  pass is for the writer; revision is where the reader comes in.

## Revising

- **Cut ten percent.** King's formula, from a rejection slip he kept: the
  second draft is the first draft minus ten percent. The number is a
  heuristic for a writer who does not yet trust their own cutting; the cuts
  themselves are in [`writing` § Revising](writing.md#revising).
- **Let it cool.** An hour, a day, a week if the piece matters. Fresh eyes
  find what tired ones excused.
- **Read it aloud.** The ear catches what the eye forgives: repeated words,
  tangled clauses, a sentence you cannot breathe through. For fiction, where
  sound is part of the work, see `story-writing`.
