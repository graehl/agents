# Story writing

> Narrative craft for fiction of any length, from a page to a novel or
> screenplay: premise and stakes, character want and need, structure models
> (three-act, hero's journey, beat sheets, story circle, kishōtenketsu),
> scene mechanics, dialogue, point of view, world-building, revision, and an
> inventory of the classic plot catalogs (Booker's 7, Tobias's 20, Polti's 36,
> Vonnegut's shapes) for finding the story's engine.

Topic: `story-writing`

Read this before planning, drafting, or revising a story, screenplay,
interactive fiction, or a character or world document. Sentence-level craft
is in [`writing`](writing.md); where the story's files go is in
[`story-project-layout`](story-project-layout.md). Use the catalogs here as a
menu for diagnosing or seeding a story, never as a form to fill for every
piece. A short story needs a premise, a character who wants something, an
obstacle, and a turn; it does not need fifteen beats.

Sources distilled here: Aristotle's *Poetics*; Freytag; Campbell via
Vogler's *The Writer's Journey*; Egri's *The Art of Dramatic Writing*;
Swain's *Techniques of the Selling Writer*; McKee's *Story*; Truby's
*The Anatomy of Story*; Snyder's *Save the Cat!*; Syd Field's *Screenplay*;
Booker's *The Seven Basic Plots*; Tobias's *20 Master Plots*; Polti's *The
Thirty-Six Dramatic Situations*; Vonnegut's story shapes and rules; Le Guin's
*Steering the Craft*; Gardner's *The Art of Fiction*; Card's *Characters and
Viewpoint*; Sanderson's lectures on magic and on promise, progress, payoff;
Pixar's 22 story rules as collected by Emma Coats; Lester Dent's pulp
formula; Kenn Adams's story spine.

## Content default: all ages

Write for a general audience unless the author says otherwise. Concretely: no
sexual content and no eroticism; a romance thread, if the author wants one,
stays at the level of a general-audience film; violence at the level the genre
needs, shown for its consequence rather than as spectacle; villains can be
frightening and losses can be real. Do not drift toward romance or adult
content as a default genre or "spice"; when offering genre options, reach for
adventure, mystery, fantasy, science fiction, comedy, survival, sports,
school and friendship, historical, ghost story, heist, and the like. The
author chooses the genre and sets the bar; the agent does not raise it on
its own.

## The engine of a story

A story is somebody who wants something badly and is having trouble getting
it. Every catalog below is a variation on that sentence.

- **Protagonist:** whose story this is. Usually the person with the most to
  lose and the most to decide.
- **Want:** the concrete, external goal the character pursues (win the race,
  find the sister, get home). Vonnegut: every character should want something,
  even if it is only a glass of water.
- **Need:** the internal thing the character actually requires, often not
  known to them (to trust someone, to stop running, to accept a loss). The
  gap between want and need is where the arc lives.
- **Obstacle or antagonist:** the force that makes the want hard. Best when it
  has its own logic and its own want; a villain who is right from their own
  seat is stronger than one who is merely evil.
- **Stakes:** what is lost if the want fails. If nothing is lost, nothing is
  at stake and the reader relaxes. Raise stakes by making them personal, then
  wider, then irreversible.
- **Turn:** the story is over when the character gets what they want, loses
  it, or discovers they wanted the wrong thing. Endings that satisfy are both
  surprising and, in retrospect, inevitable.

Egri's **premise** compresses the engine into one causal sentence: "great
love defies even death"; "ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction";
"honesty defeats duplicity." A story that cannot state its premise tends to
wander.

The **logline** is the sales-side version: *When [inciting event], a
[protagonist with a defining trait] must [want] before [stakes], but
[obstacle].* Write it before the outline and rewrite it after the draft; if
the two disagree, the draft has found a better story or lost the one it had.

**Kenn Adams's story spine** is the fastest way from nothing to a shape, and
works at every age:

> Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day ___. Because of
> that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___. And ever since then,
> ___.

The "because of that" chain is the whole secret: events connected by
consequence, not by "and then." Trey Parker and Matt Stone put it as: the
beats should be joined by "therefore" or "but," never by "and then."

## Structure models

These are lenses, not laws. Pick the one that fits the story's size and shape;
use a second only to diagnose a sag.

### Three acts

Setup (about the first quarter), confrontation (the middle half), resolution
(the last quarter). The act breaks are decisions the protagonist cannot take
back. Syd Field's screenplay version adds a **plot point** at each break and
a **midpoint** reversal. The classic sag is the second act: fix it by making
the midpoint change the nature of the problem, not just its size.

### Freytag's pyramid

Exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, dénouement. Old and
still useful for seeing where the climax actually sits; most modern stories
put it near the end, leaving a short fall.

### Hero's journey (Campbell as simplified by Vogler)

1. Ordinary world. 2. Call to adventure. 3. Refusal of the call. 4. Meeting
the mentor. 5. Crossing the first threshold. 6. Tests, allies, enemies.
7. Approach to the inmost cave. 8. Ordeal. 9. Reward. 10. The road back.
11. Resurrection. 12. Return with the elixir.

Strong for quests and coming-of-age; weak for stories that stay home. The
"refusal" and "return" steps are the ones new writers skip and readers miss.

### Dan Harmon's story circle

A hero's journey folded into eight steps that fit a short story or an
episode: 1. You (a character in a zone of comfort). 2. Need (wants
something). 3. Go (enters an unfamiliar situation). 4. Search (adapts to it).
5. Find (gets what they wanted). 6. Take (pays a heavy price). 7. Return
(goes back to the familiar situation). 8. Change (having changed).

### Save the Cat beat sheet (Snyder)

Fifteen beats with rough positions in a 110-page screenplay or a novel of
equivalent proportion. Opening image (1%). Theme stated (5%). Setup (1–10%).
Catalyst (10%). Debate (10–20%). Break into two (20%). B story (22%). Fun and
games (20–50%): the promise of the premise. Midpoint (50%): false victory or
false defeat, stakes raised. Bad guys close in (50–75%). All is lost (75%),
often with a "whiff of death." Dark night of the soul (75–80%). Break into
three (80%): the A and B stories combine into the solution. Finale (80–99%).
Final image (100%), mirroring the opening image to show change.

Most useful for screenplays and plot-driven novels; overkill for a short.

### Scene and sequel (Swain)

The smallest structural unit that reliably works. A **scene**: goal, conflict,
disaster (the character tries for something, meets opposition, ends worse or
different). A **sequel**: reaction, dilemma, decision (the character absorbs
it, weighs the bad options, chooses the next goal). Alternate them; a draft
that drags is usually all sequel, one that exhausts is all scene.

The related **try/fail cycle**: each attempt ends "yes, but" (succeeds with a
new complication) or "no, and" (fails and things get worse). Two or three
cycles before the climax make the final success feel earned.

### Promise, progress, payoff (Sanderson)

Every thread makes a promise in its opening (tone, genre, the question it
will answer), shows progress the reader can feel (a map filling in, a
relationship shifting, a mystery narrowing), and pays off what was promised.
Most reader disappointment is a payoff that answered a promise the story did
not make, or a promise never paid.

### MICE quotient (Card)

What kind of story is this, and therefore where does it begin and end?
**Milieu**: the character enters a world and leaves it. **Idea**: a question
is raised and answered. **Character**: a person is dissatisfied with their
role and finds a new one. **Event**: the world's order is broken and restored
or replaced. Nest them (a milieu story containing an idea story) and close
them in reverse order of opening.

### Kishōtenketsu

A four-part East Asian shape with no required conflict: *ki* introduction,
*shō* development, *ten* twist or a new element that recontextualizes what
came before, *ketsu* reconciliation. Good for quiet, slice-of-life, and
comic four-panel forms; a reminder that not every story is a fight.

### Lester Dent's pulp formula

For a fast 6,000-word action story in four quarters: introduce hero and
trouble and land him in it; more trouble and a first physical conflict; the
hero appears finished; the hero pulls out by his own wits, the twist is
revealed, and the ending lands with a punch. Every quarter ends with a
reversal. Crude and effective.

## Plot catalogs

Use these when a story has no engine yet, or when it has one and the writer
wants to see its relatives. Most real stories combine two or three.

### Booker's seven basic plots

1. **Overcoming the monster:** a threat must be defeated (Beowulf, *Jaws*).
2. **Rags to riches:** an overlooked person rises, loses it, and earns it
   back (Cinderella, *Aladdin*).
3. **The quest:** a journey toward a goal with companions and trials
   (*The Odyssey*, *The Lord of the Rings*).
4. **Voyage and return:** a strange world is entered and escaped, the
   traveler changed (*Alice in Wonderland*, *The Wizard of Oz*).
5. **Comedy:** confusion and misunderstanding resolved in reunion
   (*A Midsummer Night's Dream*, most romantic and farce plots).
6. **Tragedy:** a flaw or choice leads a protagonist down to ruin (*Macbeth*).
7. **Rebirth:** a protagonist frozen by a flaw or curse is freed
   (*A Christmas Carol*, *Beauty and the Beast*).

### Tobias's twenty master plots

1. Quest. 2. Adventure (the journey is the point, not the goal). 3. Pursuit
(chase). 4. Rescue. 5. Escape. 6. Revenge. 7. The riddle (mystery).
8. Rivalry. 9. Underdog. 10. Temptation. 11. Metamorphosis (a literal
transformation, often lifted by love or understanding). 12. Transformation
(a person changes through a life passage). 13. Maturation (coming of age).
14. Love. 15. Forbidden love. 16. Sacrifice. 17. Discovery (a search for the
truth about oneself). 18. Wretched excess (a character's undoing by their
own extreme). 19. Ascension (rise). 20. Descension (fall).

Tobias splits them into plots of the body (action: 1–7) and plots of the
mind (character: the rest). Numbers 14 and 15 are legitimate engines; under
the all-ages default they run on devotion and loyalty, not on desire.

### Polti's thirty-six dramatic situations

Polti (1895) cataloged the situations he found in classical and modern drama.
Each names its required elements. The list is complete here because it is
the standard reference; several entries turn on adultery or fatal passion,
and for general-audience work the same engine transposes cleanly to loyalty,
ambition, friendship, or family, as noted.

1. **Supplication:** a persecutor, a suppliant, and a power whose decision
   is uncertain.
2. **Deliverance:** an unfortunate, a threatener, a rescuer.
3. **Crime pursued by vengeance:** an avenger and a criminal.
4. **Vengeance taken for kin upon kin:** an avenging relative, a guilty
   relative, and the victim who links them.
5. **Pursuit:** a fugitive and punishment.
6. **Disaster:** a vanquished power, a victorious enemy or messenger.
7. **Falling prey to cruelty or misfortune:** an unfortunate and a master
   or misfortune.
8. **Revolt:** a tyrant and a conspirator.
9. **Daring enterprise:** a bold leader, an object, an adversary.
10. **Abduction:** an abductor, the abducted, a guardian.
11. **The enigma:** an interrogator, a seeker, a problem.
12. **Obtaining:** a solicitor and an adversary who refuses, or an arbitrator
    and opposing parties.
13. **Enmity of kin:** a hated relative and a hating relative.
14. **Rivalry of kin:** a preferred relative, a rejected relative, an object.
15. **Murderous adultery:** two adulterers and a betrayed spouse. Transposes
    to any betrayal of a bond for a rival loyalty.
16. **Madness:** a madman and a victim.
17. **Fatal imprudence:** the imprudent one, the victim or object lost.
18. **Involuntary crimes of love:** a lover, a beloved, a revealer.
    Transposes to any bond formed in ignorance of a disqualifying fact.
19. **Slaying of kin unrecognized:** a slayer and an unrecognized victim.
20. **Self-sacrifice for an ideal:** a hero, an ideal, a creditor or the
    person or thing sacrificed.
21. **Self-sacrifice for kin:** a hero, a relative, the thing sacrificed.
22. **All sacrificed for passion:** a lover, an object of passion, the thing
    sacrificed. Transposes to any consuming devotion: a craft, a cause, a
    friendship.
23. **Necessity of sacrificing loved ones:** a hero, a beloved victim, the
    necessity.
24. **Rivalry of superior and inferior:** a superior rival, an inferior
    rival, an object.
25. **Adultery:** a deceived spouse, two adulterers. Transposes to divided
    loyalty between two allies or teams.
26. **Crimes of love:** a lover and a beloved. Transposes to any wrong done
    in the name of a bond.
27. **Discovery of the dishonor of a loved one:** a discoverer, the guilty
    one.
28. **Obstacles to love:** two lovers, an obstacle. Transposes to any two
    people kept apart: friends, siblings, partners.
29. **An enemy loved:** a beloved enemy, the lover, the hater.
30. **Ambition:** an ambitious person, a thing coveted, an adversary.
31. **Conflict with a god:** a mortal, an immortal.
32. **Mistaken jealousy:** a jealous one, an object of jealousy, a supposed
    accomplice, a cause or author of the mistake.
33. **Erroneous judgment:** a mistaken one, a victim of the mistake, a cause
    or author of the mistake, the guilty one.
34. **Remorse:** a culprit, a victim or sin, an interrogator.
35. **Recovery of a lost one:** a seeker, the one found.
36. **Loss of loved ones:** a kinsman slain, a kinsman spectator, an
    executioner.

### Vonnegut's story shapes and the six arcs

Vonnegut graphed fortune against time: **Man in hole** (down, then up),
**Boy meets girl** (up, down, up), **From bad to worse**, **Which way is
up?** (ambiguous), **Creation story**, **Old Testament** (rises, then a
fall), **New Testament** (fall then infinite rise), **Cinderella** (steps up,
sudden fall, rise beyond). A 2016 sentiment analysis of 1,700 novels (Reagan
and colleagues) found six dominant arcs: rise (rags to riches), fall
(tragedy), fall then rise (man in a hole), rise then fall (Icarus), rise,
fall, rise (Cinderella), and fall, rise, fall (Oedipus). Sketch the intended
arc in one line before outlining; a flat line is the diagnosis of a boring
draft.

## Character

A character document exists to keep the writer consistent and to make the
character act, not to be admired. Keep it to what the story will use.

- **Want, need, and the lie.** The external want; the internal need; the
  false belief (Truby's "weakness," the "lie the character believes") that
  keeps them from the need. The arc is the lie being tested and, in a change
  arc, broken. In a flat arc the character already holds the truth and the
  world changes around them.
- **Ghost or wound:** the past event that installed the lie. It need not
  appear on the page; it must exist in the sheet.
- **Contradiction.** One trait that pulls against the dominant one (the
  brave one who is afraid of the dark; the liar who keeps one promise
  absolutely). Flat characters have one note; alive ones have a tension.
- **Skill and flaw.** What they are good at that the plot will need; what
  they do badly that the plot will punish.
- **Voice.** How they talk: sentence length, vocabulary, what they never say,
  what they say when scared. Two characters who could swap lines are one
  character.
- **Relationships.** Who they love, owe, resent, protect, and how each
  relationship changes by the end. Relationship change is the most legible
  form of arc.
- **Name and silhouette.** Distinct initial letters and syllable counts
  across the cast; a physical or habitual tag the reader can recognize on
  return.
- **The antagonist gets the same sheet.** Their want must be as concrete as
  the hero's and, from their seat, justified.

Give the reader at least one character to root for (Vonnegut), and be willing
to make awful things happen to them so the reader can see what they are made
of.

## Scene craft

- **Start late, leave early.** Enter the scene as close to the conflict as
  possible; exit on the turn, not on the goodbye.
- **Every scene changes something.** A value flips (safe to unsafe, ignorant
  to knowing, together to apart). If nothing has changed by the end, cut or
  merge the scene (McKee).
- **Show, don't tell, where it matters.** Dramatize the moments that carry
  emotion and decision; summarize the travel and the routine. Le Guin's
  "crowding and leaping": pack the important moments densely, leap over the
  rest.
- **Scene versus summary.** Scene is real time with dialogue and action;
  summary compresses. A draft that is all scene is long and slow; all summary
  is a synopsis.
- **Sensory anchor.** One or two specific details a scene: the sound, the
  smell, the thing out of place. Not a catalog.
- **Chekhov's gun.** A thing given weight on the page must matter later; a
  thing that matters later must have been placed earlier. Track both in the
  continuity sheet.
- **Setups and payoffs.** A twist the reader could not have seen is a cheat;
  one they could have seen and did not is a delight. Plant, then pay.
- **Pacing by sentence.** Short sentences and paragraphs accelerate; long
  ones slow and deepen. White space is speed.
- **Openings.** Start with motion, a voice, or a question; establish who, where,
  and what is wrong within the first page. Do not open with weather or with a
  character waking up unless the story is about those.
- **Endings.** Echo the opening image or line with a change; answer the
  story question; stop. Do not explain the theme.

## Dialogue

- Dialogue is action in disguise: each line is a move, wanting something from
  the other speaker. Lines that only convey information sound like a
  brochure.
- Subtext: people rarely say what they mean. Let the reader hear the meaning
  under the words.
- "Said" is invisible; use it. Adverbs on dialogue tags ("she said angrily")
  usually mean the line failed to be angry.
- Action beats instead of tags: "She put the cup down. 'No.'"
- No "As you know, Bob": characters do not tell each other what both already
  know.
- Read it aloud, in the character's voice. Cut what an actor would refuse to
  say.
- Dialect and phonetic spelling sparingly; word choice and rhythm carry
  voice better than apostrophes.

## Point of view and tense

Choose once and hold it.

- **First person:** intimate, limited, voice-driven; the narrator may be
  wrong.
- **Third limited:** the default modern choice; one head per scene, the
  narrator knows only what that character knows.
- **Third omniscient:** the narrator knows all and may comment; harder to
  keep from feeling distant; head-hopping within a scene is the common
  failure.
- **Second person:** rare, strong for interactive fiction and short
  experimental work.
- **Past tense** is the default and invisible; **present tense** feels
  immediate and is common in screenplays and some young-adult fiction.

Record the choice in the style sheet. A point-of-view slip (a scene in Ana's
head that reports what Ben is thinking) is the most common continuity error
in drafts.

## Setting and world-building

- **Start from the story, not the encyclopedia.** Build what the plot will
  touch; the rest can stay implied. The iceberg: the reader senses the mass
  below because the visible part is consistent.
- **Rules and costs.** Magic, technology, and institutions need limits the
  reader understands. Sanderson's laws: the reader accepts a problem solved
  by a power in proportion to how well they understood that power
  beforehand; limitations are more interesting than powers; expand what
  already exists before adding something new.
- **Consequences.** A world with dragons has dragon-proof roofs. Follow each
  invented fact one step into daily life; that step is where the world
  becomes believable.
- **Reveal through need.** Information arrives when a character needs it,
  through action or a conflict, never as a lecture. If a paragraph could be
  headed "Background," dramatize or cut it.
- **Names.** Consistent phonology within a culture; distinct across
  cultures; pronounceable by the reader.
- **Keep a ledger.** Every fact stated in prose (distance, date, who knows
  what, the color of the door) goes in the continuity sheet with where it was
  stated. Drafts contradict themselves; the ledger catches it.
- **Maps and timelines** only when the story moves through space or time in
  ways the reader must track. A timeline is cheap and prevents the most
  embarrassing errors.
- **Beware worldbuilder's disease:** building the world is more fun than
  writing the story, and is not the story.

## Screenplay basics

- One page is roughly one minute of screen time. A feature runs about 90 to
  120 pages; a short, 5 to 15.
- Only what can be seen or heard goes on the page: no thoughts, no
  backstory, no "she remembers."
- **Scene heading:** `INT.` or `EXT.`, location, `DAY` or `NIGHT`. **Action**
  lines in present tense, short paragraphs. **Character name** in capitals
  above each speech; **parentheticals** sparingly; **transitions** rarely.
- Enter late, leave early applies doubly. Dialogue is shorter than in prose.
- Write in Fountain (plain-text screenplay markup) or an equivalent so the
  script stays in ordinary files; format is convertible later.
- Outline as a **treatment** (prose synopsis, a few pages) before the script;
  a **beat sheet** between the two for anything feature length.

## Interactive fiction

A branching story is a graph of scenes. Keep the graph small and the
branches meaningful: a choice that leads to the same next scene is
decoration, not a choice. Track state (what the reader has done, knows,
holds) explicitly; every branch must be reachable and every ending must close
the story question. Give the "bad" endings dignity; they are still endings.

## Habits of machine-written prose to avoid

These are the tells a reader uses to spot generated fiction. They are
defaults an assistant drifts into unprompted, so check for them on every
pass, not only when asked.

- Opening on weather, dawn light, or a character waking; opening with a
  catalog of sensory detail before anything happens.
- The three-item list as a reflex ("cold, dark, and silent"), and the
  reversal cadence ("it wasn't the fall that scared her, it was the
  silence").
- Em-dash pileups and rhetorical fragments used for weight. Short sentences.
  Like this. Everywhere.
- Every character speaking in the same articulate register; children and
  villains who talk like essayists.
- Emotion named instead of shown ("she felt a wave of grief"), and a
  paragraph explaining what the scene already showed.
- Scenes that resolve neatly, characters who agree, obstacles that yield on
  the first try; no "no, and."
- Endings that state the theme, tie every thread, or close on a wise
  sentence about what was learned.
- Stock phrases: "a testament to," "a reminder that," "in that moment," "little
  did they know," "couldn't help but," eyes that "sparkle with mischief."
- Inflated vocabulary where a plain word fits ("tapestry," "whisper" for
  anything quiet, "dance" for anything moving).
- Names from the same shortlist (Elara, Kael, Lyra, Aria). Pick names the
  writer suggests or ones with a reason.

## Revision

- **Finish the draft first.** A complete bad draft is worth more than a
  perfect first act. Do not revise chapter one until the end exists, because
  the end will change what chapter one must set up.
- **Read the whole thing through** before touching anything, noting only
  "boring," "confusing," "don't believe it," and "good."
- **Structural pass:** does the logline still describe the book? Does each
  act end on an irreversible decision? Is there a midpoint turn? Are the
  setups paid and the payoffs set up? Does the protagonist drive the climax,
  or is it done to them?
- **Character pass:** does each character want something in each scene? Does
  the protagonist's lie get tested and resolved? Could any two characters
  merge?
- **Scene pass:** what changes in each scene? Cut the scene that changes
  nothing; combine two that change the same thing.
- **Line pass:** apply [`writing`](writing.md). Cut ten percent. Kill
  adverbs. Read aloud. Sweep the machine-prose tells above.
- **Continuity pass:** walk the ledger against the draft.
- **A reader.** One who will say where they got bored or confused, not what
  to do about it. Take the diagnosis; ignore the prescriptions (the Neil
  Gaiman rule).

## Working with a new or young writer

The goal is a finished story they are proud of, in their voice. Craft
vocabulary is for the agent's diagnosis, not for the writer's homework.

1. **Interview briefly.** Who is it about? What do they want? What is in the
   way? Where does it happen? How should it feel at the end (happy, sad,
   scary, funny)? Five questions, then stop asking.
2. **Reflect a logline or spine** in their words and ask if it is right.
   If they hesitate, riff: four independent alternatives from the same
   brief, each seeded differently, labeled A to D with a one-line
   description, plus a recommendation. They pick or name what to combine.
   Where a `riff` skill is available, use it; otherwise do it by hand and
   say the four share one context. This is the default way to propose at
   every creative choice point below: the opening, a character's voice, the
   ending, a title.
3. **Write a short version first.** A page or a spine-length telling. Read it
   back. Ask what is the best part and what is boring. Their answers set the
   plan.
4. **Expand from the best part.** Grow the draft toward what excites them;
   let the boring part shrink or vanish.
5. **Keep their ideas.** When an idea does not fit, find the version of it
   that does before dropping it. Their weird detail is often the story's
   best asset.
6. **Praise specifically, fix one thing.** Name the line that worked. Offer
   the one change that most improves the next draft.
7. **Finish.** Reach an ending, even a rough one, before polishing. Then, if
   they want, grow it: a story into chapters, chapters into a book.
8. **Length ladder.** A paragraph, then a page, then a scene with a turn, then
   a chapter. Do not start a twelve-year-old on a fifteen-beat sheet.
